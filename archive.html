Messy files → one source of truth

5shared drives audited, file by file
~150true duplicates cleared, nothing lost
12 → 1competing org charts down to one

The problem

Years of policies, contracts and records were spread across five SharePoint sites. Nobody could tell which copy of a document was the real one. There were about a dozen versions of the org chart and six of the employee handbook.

What I did

  • Designed a simple four-level folder system where where a file lives tells you whether it's current or archived, so filenames stay clean.
  • Set a strict rule: two files only count as duplicates if their actual contents match, not just their names. That caught look-alikes that were really different.
  • Rebuilt everything around the real departments, and wrote nine short reference guides that answer "what's true?" in one place.
  • Spotted a security risk along the way (passwords stored in plain text) and reported it.

What changed

People stopped guessing. Leadership now works from one verified copy of everything, and there's a clear schedule for what to keep and what to delete.

AI · Policy

Writing the company's AI policy

12 of 13staff answered the survey
7rounds of feedback before adoption
7stakeholder meetings I ran

The problem

Staff were already using AI tools, with no rules about what's safe, what data can go in, or who approves new accounts.

What I did

  • Surveyed staff on what tools they used, for what, and what worried them. Average comfort with AI was 4 out of 5, so the job was guardrails, not persuasion.
  • Turned each survey finding into a specific policy decision, then wrote the AI Use Policy and took it through seven rounds of review.
  • Replaced "just ask someone" with a simple request form, so every AI account has a reason and a sign-off.
  • Wrote a plain-language FAQ, training guides and a multi-year budget for the tools.

What changed

The organization went from informal AI use to an adopted policy with a real approval process, trained staff and a budget it can plan around.

Web app

Turning a strategic plan into a live app

34 for 34strategies matched the official plan
~4,800lines of custom code, directed by me
3views: staff, executives, admins

The problem

The board-approved strategic plan, with goals broken into strategies, tasks and targets, lived in spreadsheets. It was hard to see who owned what, what was late, or how to report progress to the board.

What I did

  • Wrote the full blueprint: what data the app holds, who can see and edit what, and what every screen does.
  • Directed the build with Claude Code and reviewed every iteration myself. Stack: Next.js, Prisma, PostgreSQL, Vercel.
  • Loaded the real plan and checked each of the 34 strategies against the official document. All 34 matched, with no errors.
  • Added what staff actually needed: a "what's due" banner with one-click submit, an Excel importer so nobody re-types the planning template, and a tracker showing who has submitted.
  • Designed board-ready dashboards grounded in proven formats (balanced scorecards, bullet graphs) that clearly separate real numbers from estimates.

What changed

Leadership can see the whole plan, who owns each piece and what's behind, in one place, ready for the year-end board presentation.

Money saved

Catching a vendor overbilling us

~20%off the monthly IT bill, ongoing
$0gap between the bill and the contract after

The problem

Nobody had checked the IT company's bills against the contract in years. User counts were unclear and the rates didn't add up.

What I did

  • Read the contract and compared it to every invoice, one line at a time.
  • Found two problems: the wrong rate was being charged, and we were paying for more users and mailboxes than we had.
  • Worked with the vendor to fix the bill and refund the extra charges.
  • Built a cost model that assigns each charge to the right department, so budgets are accurate going forward.

What changed

A bill that matches the contract to the dollar, about 20% lower every month, and a clean paper trail for the next renewal.

Product thinking

Designing an employee portal people would actually use

19 → 8features cut to what has real content
3menu tabs, down from a long list
1 yearbefore any how-to guide must be re-approved

The problem

Policies, how-to guides and forms were scattered, and most department guides were marked "needs update" with nobody responsible for them.

What I did

  • Started big (19 possible features), then cut to the 8 with real content behind them. Everything else was parked with a reason, not half-built.
  • Researched how good company intranets are organized and simplified the menu to three tabs. Your own department's policies and guides show up first.
  • Gave every department one named owner for its guides. Approved guides expire after a year and land back in the owner's queue, so nothing silently goes stale.
  • Chose calm over alarm: a simple "2 overdue" count instead of red-yellow-green gauges, and search that tags documents automatically so staff never have to.

What changed

One calm, simple place for staff to find what they need, with ownership built in so it stays current.

Data · Impact

Proving a youth STEM program was working

+9%rise in STEM confidence after workshops
1,377student surveys combined
33schools across 7 districts

The problem

A program introducing San Antonio students to STEM careers had years of data spread across partial, inconsistent spreadsheets, so there was no way to show its impact.

What I did

  • Combined everything into one clean dataset, and caught a file where the columns had shifted. Left alone, that would have quietly scrambled the results.
  • Compared students' before-and-after ratings. Confidence and interest in STEM rose from 3.65 to 3.99 out of 5.
  • Mapped where students live by ZIP code to show which neighborhoods the program reaches.
  • Removed all personal information from every file I worked with or delivered.

What changed

The organization's first clear, error-checked picture of who the program serves and the difference it makes, ready to share with funders and the board.
