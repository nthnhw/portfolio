Organization

Make information trustworthy

5shared drives audited
100+duplicates retired, nothing lost
1source of truth

The situation

An organization's documents had built up for years across several shared drives. Nobody could be sure which version of anything was current.

My approach

  • Built a simple folder system where a file's location tells you whether it's current or archived.
  • Treated two files as duplicates only when their actual contents matched, not just their names.
  • Reorganized everything around how the organization really works, and wrote short guides to what's true.
  • Flagged a security risk I found along the way.

The result

One trusted place for records, with clear rules for what to keep and what to retire.

Takeaway: Cleanup only lasts if the structure makes the right thing the easy thing.

AI · Policy

Listen first, then write the rules

90%+of staff surveyed
5+rounds of feedback
1simple approval process

The situation

Staff were already using AI tools, with no shared rules about safety, data or approvals.

My approach

  • Surveyed staff on what they used, why, and what worried them.
  • Turned what I heard into specific policy decisions, then refined the policy through several rounds of feedback.
  • Replaced informal approvals with a simple request process.
  • Wrote plain-language training and a FAQ so the policy was usable, not just official.

The result

An adopted AI policy with a working approval process and staff who understand it.

Takeaway: People follow rules that reflect how they actually work.

Systems

Turn plans into something people use

30+strategies in one app
100%of records checked against the plan
3views: staff, leaders, admins

The situation

A strategic plan lived in spreadsheets, so it was hard to see who owned what or how things were going.

My approach

  • Wrote the blueprint: what the tool tracks, who sees what, and what each screen is for.
  • Directed the build of a custom web app and reviewed every version myself.
  • Checked every record against the official plan before anyone relied on it.
  • Added what made it easy to use, like clear deadlines and importing from the existing template.

The result

Leadership and staff can see the whole plan, who owns each part, and where things stand, all in one place.

Takeaway: A tool gets used when it answers each person's first question: what's mine, and what's next?

Operations

The savings are in the details

~20%lower monthly cost
100%of invoice lines checked
$0gap between bill and contract after

The situation

A long-running vendor relationship had never been checked against its contract.

My approach

  • Compared the contract to the invoices, line by line.
  • Found billing errors and worked with the vendor to correct them and recover the overcharges.
  • Built a simple model that assigns costs to the right departments going forward.

The result

A lower, accurate bill and a clean record for future renewals.

Takeaway: Nobody notices when a bill quietly drifts from the contract. A careful check pays for itself every month after.

Product thinking

Less, but better

~60%of the wish list cut
3menu tabs
1 yrreview cycle for every guide

The situation

Staff needed one place for policies, how-to guides and forms, and many guides had no clear owner.

My approach

  • Started with a long wish list, then kept only the features with real content behind them.
  • Simplified the navigation so people see their own department's material first.
  • Gave every guide an owner, and made approvals expire so content gets reviewed instead of going stale.
  • Chose calm, simple signals over dashboards that make everything feel urgent.

The result

A focused, easy-to-use portal with upkeep built into how it works.

Takeaway: Cutting scope is a design decision. What you leave out keeps the rest usable.

Data · Impact

Make good work visible

1,000+student surveys combined
30+schools reached
~10%rise in STEM confidence

The situation

A youth STEM program had years of data spread across inconsistent files, so its impact was hard to show.

My approach

  • Combined the data into one clean set and caught an error that would have skewed the results.
  • Compared students' before-and-after responses to measure the program's effect.
  • Mapped which communities the program reaches.
  • Removed personal information from everything I worked with.

The result

A clear, checked picture of who the program serves and the difference it makes.

Takeaway: Good programs often lack the data to prove it. That story is worth digging out.

Research · TAMUSA

Research

Memory, Attention & Perception Lab at Texas A&M University–San Antonio, with Dr. Dawn Weatherford. The big question: how do people decide whether a face matches an ID, and why do they get it wrong?

Doing two things at once

Does multitasking make people worse at checking IDs? We tracked their eye movements to see where mistakes come from. This won first place at the 2024 TAMUSA graduate competition.

A better face database

Mapping how similar faces look across cameras, emotions, time and settings, so researchers have more realistic test material.

Where eyes go during ID checks

Can eye-tracking predict who will catch a fake ID? Presented at the International Association for Craniofacial Identification and SWPA.

Lighting and memory

Does the lighting you saw a face in help you remember it later? Analysis is complete and the paper is in progress.
